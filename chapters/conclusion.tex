\section{Conclusion}

Alfred Snider first recognized debate as a game and championed its transformative power \parencite{snider1982gaming,snider1984gaming}, yet none who followed applied game-design know-how to the question his argument makes inevitable: how can we make it a better game? This thesis takes one step into that question.

This thesis answers a ``how can'' question in three stages: whether the conceptual tools apply (Sections \ref{sec:background}--\ref{sec:theory}: debate is a transformative role-playing game and game design can be brought to bear on it); whether a concrete design can be carried through (Sections \ref{sec:methods}--\ref{sec:design}: the Inside Out format and its iterative development); and whether the attempt produced value (Results and Discussion: clue-by-clue confirmation and theory-facing extensions).

On \textit{value convergence}: Field~5 confirms the value-pluralism shift via memetic bleed, with the second-session bleed event as the dataset's single highest-strength observation. On \textit{information density}: Field~3 confirms transferable persuasion, with audience adaptation, second-person address, and worldbuilding all emerging unscripted. On \textit{competitive hierarchy}: Field~4 confirms container safety within the playtest window; persistence under maturation is what the next iteration would test.

To my own body of knowledge this thesis contributes most in becoming an artifact of academic frustration. Although Inside Out does constitute a first and not strictly necessary step in a much longer process of reform, whose later stages involve refinement, community adoption, governance design, and the slow work of shifting collective habits, the utility of the scholarly wrapper as part of which it was developed fails yet to shine bright enough to warrant the effort put in constructing it. This is because the deductive arc this thesis narrates is but a necessary pretence. The actual design emerged intuitively and was retrofitted to theory; that it held testifies to the scholarly aplication of the argumentative skills debate built in me, not to the merits of this theory-born deductive design methodology. One cannot but wonder how far the design would have evolved if effort were to have been invested in testing and refining and not in overcoming the difficulty of finding only over-18 playtesters and in wordsmithing convoluted theoretical analysis.

What this thesis carries forward, then, is neither a finished format nor great insights but three boons for whose utility one can argue:
- my emotional momentum required to pursue the subsequent stages of reform that the design itself cannot deliver;
- misguided yet instrumentalizable academic legitimization of this format and my scholarly self, a tool for creating alibi of play and funding opportunities;
- a wanton dream of such research being picked up by those rare colleagues that find themselves also at the identity intersection of debate heritage, game-design career and scholarly ambitions. 

And with that the drums of ambition beat to the march of dreams to persevere in research galore, to fund and discover how to construct a financially sustainable organism that orchestrates and supports debate-centric educational gameplay. Of this march, the first steps I spoke of at the end of the discussion. May there be many more to come. ``Tuna'' proved that one can fund a lifestyle of meaningful teaching helping hundreds of young people simply by writing academic papers on how debate is a game. One can hope this lightning strikes twice.
