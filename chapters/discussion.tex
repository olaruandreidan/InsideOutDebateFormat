\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The results chapter read the playtests, clue by clue, against the predictions written into the design. This chapter does something different. It returns to the central claim of Section~\ref{sec:background} --- that competitive debate is a member of the role-playing-game family --- and asks what follows from accepting it. Two arguments are made in turn. Section~\ref{sec:rpg-insights} reads the playtest evidence through the broadened category and identifies four places where including debate sharpens existing constructs in role-playing-game studies. Section~\ref{sec:further-study} identifies the directions the results point toward for continued study, and argues for each that the gap it names is general to the transformative-game-design field rather than a particularity of debate.

\subsection{What Including Debate Reveals About Role-Playing Games}
\label{sec:rpg-insights}

Section~\ref{sec:background} argued that academic debate satisfies the functional definitions of role-playing games offered by \textcite{zagal2024definitions} and \textcite{bowman2025transformative}, and that it operates through the same psychological mechanisms: character immersion via epistemic-stance assumption, alibi for identity experimentation, and a discourse community that sustains the activity beyond the round. The playtests give that argument an analytic afterlife rather than only a taxonomic one. Debate is an unusually constrained, adult-instrumented member of the family: its rules are tight, its fictional latitude narrow, and its participants enter under explicit instrumental rationales. Those constraints make visible aspects of role-playing structure that the fictional latitude of LARP usually keeps tacit. The four refinements that follow are, accordingly, generalisations back to role-playing-game studies, not local lessons about debate.

The framework currently treats alibi as a single concept appearing under two complementary glosses. \textcite{deterding2017alibis} names the \textit{justification-alibi}: the rationale an adult adopts as licence to play in the first place. \textcite{bowman2025transformative} names the \textit{performance-alibi}: the in-play permission to act, decide, and feel under cover of character. The Inside Out playtests show these are not parallel but coupled. The Rolling Out closing ritual --- a framing device of the kind transformative LARP uses without difficulty --- was reported as ``robotic'' in the first session and met with mumbling in the second. Yet the same participants spontaneously adopted ``Inner Voices'' as in-play address. Both moves were formally licensed by the same in-play permission. What discriminated them was justification: chanting felt decorative under a rationale that read play as ``learning argumentation,'' but natural where the rationale was ``inhabiting a character.'' The justification an adult brings to the table places a prior on the performance-alibis they will exercise once seated. A framing device that is procedurally correct in one role-playing context can fail in another simply because the format's adult-justification rules out the registers it operates in. The contribution to the framework is to treat the adult-justification surface not as a separate designable surface from the in-play one, but as a filter on it.

The second refinement concerns evaluator roles. Reform discourse around debate's value-convergence problem has long treated judges as neutral arbiters in need of better calibration. The role-playing-game framing identifies a different mechanism. Standard formats ask every judge to roleplay the same archetype --- the average, unbiased person --- and that uniformity, not demographic homogeneity, is what produces drift: the role itself pulls interpretation toward a single imagined audience and makes deviation feel like error. Inside Out's structural choices act on this directly. The Interlocutor roleplays a specific named character; there are three teams; persuasion targets values rather than policy; and the S0 speech grants the Subject worldbuilding licence, which provides an explicit alibi to interpret the same character differently across rounds. Second-playtest debriefs captured the mechanism almost verbatim, contrasting the S0 framing of bias as perspective with what participants called the ``pretense of objectivity'' in standard formats. Generalised, the claim is that any structurally privileged interpretive role --- judge, GM, table audience, evaluator --- exerts homogenising pressure proportional to how unitary the role itself is, regardless of who fills it. The role's psychological specificity is therefore a first-order design variable, not a community-support afterthought. This is also the cleanest demonstration of the thesis's methodological claim: only treating the judge as a roleplayed character names the parameter that needed redesign, where the standard reform vocabulary of bias and calibration does not.

The third refinement concerns the dual stance evaluative roles demand of those who hold them. The Subjects in the two playtests responded to their role differently. The first described not knowing ``what to say,'' caught between emotional investment and evaluative neutrality. The second resolved the same tension by leaning into the roleplay rather than against it: inhabiting the character, responding emotionally, and asking from genuine curiosity. The framework as written has no construct for what the second Subject did. Bleed describes what flows into the player; alibi describes what the social frame permits; neither captures the player's ongoing capacity to inhabit a character while retaining an external stance on what the scene is doing. The Nordic theory of \textit{steering} names exactly this \parencite{montola2015steering, pohjola2015steering}. The Subject's role makes plain that steering is not a Nordic-LARP particularity. Any role-playing game whose participants must hold an evaluative stance alongside character inhabitation calls on the same capacity, which means transformative formats should treat steering as a teachable, trackable skill that facilitation architecture can plan for, rather than as a participant disposition. Importing the construct into transformative-game-design theory follows; debate's evaluative weight is what made the gap visible.

The fourth refinement concerns where pressure comes from once external competition has been removed. The framework anticipated that removing win/loss outcomes would require a replacement source of competitive motivation, and the commendation architecture was designed to provide it. The playtests instead surfaced a different mechanism. Pressure was present and productive in both sessions; its operative source was peer regard. Participants wanted recognition from fellow Inner Voices and from the Subject for the quality and resonance of their contributions, and effort tracked that desire more closely than it tracked the formal commendation system. This extends \textcite{bogost2007persuasive}'s procedural-rhetoric account in a small but specific way: the rule that becomes operative on players need not be the rule the scoring system codifies, and a rule structure designed to reward contextual contribution can produce a social rule that rewards it more reliably than the formal evaluative channel does. Transformative-game-design theory has rich accounts of container, alibi, and bleed but thinner accounts of what sustains motivational intensity once external competition is stripped out. Peer recognition within a bounded play group is the missing third pressure category --- alongside competitive stakes and progression architecture --- and it is a designable surface in its own right. Open questions about how it scales beyond a single session are deferred to Section~\ref{sec:peer-recognition-study}.

The four refinements share a structure. Each emerges because debate is a tightly constrained, adult-instrumented role-playing game, and that constrainedness sharpens an aspect of role-playing structure that LARP's fictional latitude usually leaves tacit: the adult-justification axis of alibi, the homogenising effect of a unitary evaluator role, the steering capacity demanded by evaluative roles, and the work peer recognition does in the absence of external competition. The contribution to role-playing-game studies is therefore not the addition of a new game to the canon. It is a vantage point --- debate's analytic position relative to its cousin forms --- from which several existing constructs become more precise.

\subsection{Directions for Continued Study}
\label{sec:further-study}

The remainder of the chapter turns from refinements the playtests already support to directions they merely point toward. Each of the following four directions arises from a finding in Section~\ref{sec:results} that the Inside Out design either left underdeveloped or surfaced as a research question rather than a settled answer. For each, the gap the playtests named is general to the transformative-game-design field, not specific to debate.

\subsubsection{Progression Architecture}
\label{sec:progression}

The commendation system was received with genuine enthusiasm --- one evaluator described it as ``scratching the make-a-build itch'' --- but the same evaluators reported difficulty assigning commendations consistently, and one named the risk of ``farming'': stylised play optimised for commendation coverage rather than development. Both difficulties point to the same gap. The framework specifies what transformation \textit{is} --- bleed, identity work, memetic uptake --- but neither how a facilitator \textit{sees} it occurring in real time, nor how a progression structure can be designed so that optimising for its rewards is identical to the development the rewards are meant to register. Gamification and progressive-game-design literatures have developed substantial theory on exactly this alignment problem: reward loops, milestone structure, and the calibration of extrinsic incentives against the intrinsic capacities they are meant to cultivate. Integrating that work into transformative-game-design theory is a research direction the playtests sharpen but that the field would benefit from regardless. The gap is general; the commendation system is one instance of it.

\subsubsection{Governance as a Designable Surface}
\label{sec:governance}

The most consequential observation in the second debrief was the participants' own reconstruction of how speedtalking became British Parliamentary's meta and then contaminated World Schools after the judge pools merged. Their account was not about rules. As the founding university teachers disengaged from BP, rule interpretation fell to whoever held social power in the circuit, and that group systematically privileged the skill hardest to master because that was what sustained their standing. A new format dropped into that community inherits the same governance pathology regardless of its ruleset. \textcite{bowman2025transformative}'s ``community support'' category does not reach this. Governance --- how rules are interpreted, how evaluators are trained, who authors motions, whose voice sets the meta's drift --- is a designable surface above the format, and arguably more consequential for long-run behaviour than anything in the format specification. The community-management literature developed for online and in-person practice communities has tools transformative-game-design has not yet integrated. The applicability is general: any transformative format whose long-run behaviour depends on a sustaining community --- LARP scenes, educational role-play programmes, simulation networks --- inherits the same surface.

\subsubsection{Sustained Peer-Recognition Pressure}
\label{sec:peer-recognition-study}

Section~\ref{sec:rpg-insights} named peer recognition as a third pressure category in transformative formats. Doing so opens questions the playtests cannot answer. How does peer-recognition pressure scale beyond the bounded session, where every participant can attend to every other? How does it interact with the progression architecture studied in Section~\ref{sec:progression}? What failure modes does it carry: clique formation, the in-group bias a small group compounds, the same homogenising drift that a unitary evaluator role produces in standard debate? These are not debate-specific questions. They apply to any transformative format that has chosen to remove external competition and must therefore source motivational intensity elsewhere --- a design choice many such formats make, and few have studied past first-order container claims.

\subsubsection{Modularity as a Research Programme}
\label{sec:modularity}

The framework tends to treat transformative formats as integrated wholes whose components reinforce one another. Inside Out is also that, but it is additionally decomposable. Five of its design moves can be transplanted into an existing format independently of the others: the commendation system; the split between an in-debate Interlocutor whose performance is evaluated and an external Evaluator whose is not; three teams rather than two; a judge who roleplays a specific named character; and teams prescribed values but granted creative freedom over the solutions that express them. Each is a candidate for single-mechanism transplantation, which invites a research methodology in which transformative mechanisms are studied as transplantable units rather than as constituents of holistic experiences. The lens generalises beyond Inside Out. Any transformative format proposed in the literature can be analysed for which of its components are load-bearing mechanisms and which are consequences of integration. Mainstream game-design analysis already treats mechanic-level contributions in this way; importing the habit into transformative-game-design study would sharpen the field's empirical purchase.

\bigskip

The two arguments compose a single contribution. From the encounter between debate and transformative-game-design theory, the framework gains both refined constructs --- the coupling of alibi senses, the design status of evaluator roles, the importability of steering, peer recognition as a pressure category --- and a research programme that the next iteration of work, on Inside Out or elsewhere, can now pursue.
